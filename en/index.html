Sustainability

Mining as a tool of the energy transition.

Bitcoin mining is the most flexible electricity consumer in the world: we can switch hundreds of megawatts on or off within seconds. That is exactly what grids with high wind and solar shares need.

  • Avoid curtailment: We buy power that would otherwise be wasted – making renewable projects more profitable.
  • Grid-friendly operation: During peak load our facilities throttle automatically and hand capacity back to the grid.
  • Reuse waste heat: In Brandenburg our pilot plant heats a greenhouse – 70% of the waste heat is reused.
  • Report transparently: Our energy mix is published quarterly and audited externally.

Learn

Understanding mining – in four steps.

01

Collect transactions

Miners bundle unconfirmed Bitcoin transactions from the mempool into a block candidate.

02

Proof of work

Specialized chips (ASICs) try quadrillions of hash combinations per second to find a valid block hash.

03

Block found

Whoever finds a valid hash first gets to append the block and receives the block reward plus transaction fees – currently 3.125 BTC per block.

04

Secure the network

The more computing power mines worldwide, the more expensive an attack on Bitcoin becomes. Mining is the network's security layer.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

Is home mining still worth it?

In most cases not at typical European household rates (~€0.30/kWh). Profitable mining needs electricity below roughly 10–12 ct/kWh, efficient hardware, and good cooling. At home it gets interesting mainly in combination with your own solar system or when the waste heat replaces heating costs.

What is the halving and why does it matter?

Roughly every four years the block reward is cut in half. Since the 2024 halving, miners receive 3.125 BTC per block. This tightens supply on a fixed schedule and forces miners to become ever more efficient – a key driver of our technology investments.

How much electricity does Bitcoin consume – and is that a problem?

The Bitcoin network consumes roughly 150–200 TWh per year globally. What matters is not just the amount but the source: a growing share comes from renewable or otherwise stranded energy. Mining can monetize surplus power and make wind and solar buildouts more economical.

What happens if the Bitcoin price falls?

When the price falls, inefficient miners leave the network, difficulty drops, and the remaining miners earn more BTC per unit of hashrate again. This mechanism stabilizes the system. For us the rule is: whoever has the lowest power costs stays profitable the longest.
